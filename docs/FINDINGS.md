# Experiment Log — Transporting Distribution-Function Inference Across Populations

This log documents experiments on the estimation and uncertainty quantification of the poverty headcount θ_t(P) = P_P(Y < t) when it is transported to previously unseen populations. The exchangeable unit throughout is the population. Short tags used in the tables: within-population bootstrap interval (M0); random-effects variance-inflated interval (M1, Gaussian variance inflation); pointwise split-conformal band (M2, per-threshold); population conformal band (PCB); weighted conformal band (M3, covariate-shift); localized conformal band (M3′, bias-corrected); leave-one-country-out (LOCO); leave-one-survey-out (LOSO).

## Experiment 1 — Unified Mini-Benchmark (`unified_benchmark/`)

**Design:** LOSO over three surveys with a single LightGBM base predictor. Noise and calibration are fit out-of-sample only (avoiding in-sample leakage). All methods are compared against the same out-of-fold point predictions.

| Method | poverty_MAPE | fold SD |
|---|---|---|
| no_noise (point prediction) | 0.090 | 0.006 |
| ELL_t | 0.0304 | 0.011 |
| ELL_bootstrap | 0.0302 | 0.011 |
| Platt | 0.0300 | 0.014 |

**Conclusion:** (i) Distributional calibration improves on point prediction by roughly 3x. (ii) The three calibration methods are effectively tied (0.0300–0.0304); a previously reported "Platt ≫ ELL" ranking is an artifact of differing cross-validation schemes and in-sample calibration. (iii) The fold SD (0.011) far exceeds the between-method difference (0.0004), so method differences are not statistically meaningful. This is a clean reproduction of a known result (ELL 2003) with inflated claims removed.

## Experiment 2 — Cross-Population Coverage Collapse (`coverage/`)

**Design:** 24 pseudo-populations are generated by survey × strata, with leave-one-group-out evaluation. For each group's headcount we measure the actual coverage of (a) a naive within-sample bootstrap interval and (b) an interval that adds between-population variance. Nominal level is 90%.

| Measurement (24 × 19 = 456 cells) | Value |
|---|---|
| Point headcount MAE | 0.047 |
| Naive SE (within population) | 0.0061 |
| Between-population SD (shift) | 0.0412 (6.8x) |
| Naive interval coverage | 7.9% |
| Population-aware interval coverage | 74.3% |

**Conclusion:**
1. Standard-practice uncertainty collapses across populations: the naive 90% interval covers the truth only 7.9% of the time (about 7x overconfident).
2. Simple variance inflation (random-effects / jackknife-over-population) recovers most of the loss (from 7.9% to 74.3%) but falls short of 90%. The 74%–90% gap motivates transport-aware / conformal procedures.
3. The pseudo-populations share the same survey DGP, so this coverage estimate is optimistic; on genuinely new surveys coverage would be lower and the true gap larger.

## Experiment 3 — Closing the Gap with Population-Level Conformal (`coverage/step4_conformal.py`)

**Design:** Instead of Gaussian variance inflation, set the exchangeable unit to the population and construct a leave-one-population-out empirical-quantile conformal interval. Two evaluations: (A) exchange over 24 groups (optimistic), and (B) leave-one-survey-out, i.e. genuinely new surveys unseen by the prediction model (genuine shift).

| Coverage @ nominal 90% | Gaussian variance inflation | Population conformal |
|---|---|---|
| A) Group exchange (optimistic) | 73.9% | 87.5% |
| B) Genuine survey shift | 70.6% | 87.9% |

**Conclusion:**
1. Three-stage progression: the standard within-sample interval covers 7.9%, variance inflation 71–74%, and population conformal 88%. The empirical quantile captures the heavy tail and bias of the transport error.
2. The genuine-shift evaluation (B) reaches 87.9% on surveys never seen by the prediction model, nearly identical to the optimistic test (A), indicating the method works under genuine transport.
3. A ~2 percentage-point shortfall remains (88% vs 90%). With only three surveys, the conformal calibration set is effectively two populations (groups within a survey share a DGP), so exact 90% is not reached; external multi-survey / simulation extensions are needed.

**Caveats (Experiments 1–3):** All figures are validation estimates from a single base model and few populations, not a measured leaderboard. The pseudo-populations in Experiment 2 are not independent draws, so coverage is an upper-bound (optimistic) estimate. With only three real source populations, finite-sample guarantees cannot be established from this data alone.

## Experiment 4 — Synthetic Population-Hierarchy Simulation (E2) (`pcb/simulation/`)

**Design:** A synthetic population hierarchy (K source + 1 target ~ Π) with controlled K, shift, and misspecification. A fixed linear base predictor does not observe population id, so transport error arises from an unobserved intercept a_i; misspecification (a dropped quadratic term) induces bias and non-normality. Exchangeable unit = population, 80 reps per cell. Code: `generate_populations.py`, `run_sim_grid.py`.

**Methodological note.** An initial implementation reported that M3 reduced width to 0.90–0.98x. That reduction was an artifact of M3 using quantile levels α/2 and 1−α/2, which caused undercoverage. Correcting M3 to the finite-sample conformal levels (with a type-7 weighted quantile, so that under uniform weights M3 reduces to M2 to machine precision) removes the apparent width reduction; weighted reweighting then yields no efficiency gain. The figures below are the corrected values.

### K-sweep (shift = high, misspec = severe)
| K | M0 naive (predicted) | M1 var-infl | M2 conformal | M3 weighted |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 11.9% (18.2) | 57.2% | 50.6% | 50.6% |
| 5 | 6.9% (15.8) | 62.3% | 55.7% | 55.7% |
| 10 | 9.7% (13.9) | 75.4% | 73.6% | 73.6% |
| 20 | 13.7% (12.7) | 84.5% | 85.3% | 85.4% |
| 50 | 15.6% (12.3) | 90.9% | 95.3% | 95.6% |

### shift-sweep (K = 20)
| shift | ratio | M1 var-infl | M2 conformal | M3 weighted |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| none | 0.7x | 13.9% | 88.0% | 88.4% |
| low | 2.2x | 71.1% | 85.7% | 85.4% |
| medium | 5.1x | 86.8% | 86.5% | 86.4% |
| high | 9.8x | 84.5% | 85.3% | 85.4% |

### Structured vs. unstructured shift (K = 20, high)
| distance ↔ error correlation | M2 cov/width | M3 cov/width | width M3/M2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| unstructured (a_i ⟂ covariate) | 85.3% / 0.385 | 85.4% / 0.384 | 0.999 |
| structured (a_i ~ covariate mean) | 82.4% / 0.296 | 83.8% / 0.295 | 0.996 |

**Conclusion:**
1. The naive within-population bootstrap interval (M0) collapses in every cell (6–26%). A first-order prediction of the collapse magnitude is accurate at high shift and large K (K = 50: predicted 12.3 vs. observed 15.6).
2. Pointwise split-conformal coverage (M2) rises with K (51% at K = 3 to 95% at K = 50), undercovering at small K; this reproduces small-K conformal undercoverage. The ~2 pp gap with three real surveys (Experiment 3) is one instance of this phenomenon.
3. Under shift = none with severe misspecification, the random-effects variance-inflated interval (M1) covers only 13.9% versus 88.0% for M2. When transport error is bias (a dropped quadratic term), a zero-centered Gaussian is invalid while conformal captures the bias.
4. The weighted conformal band (M3, shift-weighted quantile) gives no significant width reduction at matched coverage, even under structured shift (0.996x). When shift is structured, transport error is predictable from observed covariates, and the resulting gain comes from re-centering (bias correction), not quantile reweighting; a plain weighted quantile does not move the band center, so width does not shrink. This motivates a localized, bias-corrected conformal band (Experiment 6).

This experiment shows transport error is captured by conformal but not by Gaussian variance inflation, and that quantile reweighting alone yields no efficiency gain.

## Experiment 5 — Population Conformal Bands (PCB): Finding and Method

**Motivating finding:** The pointwise split-conformal band (M2) covers each threshold at the nominal level, but the proportion covering the entire curve simultaneously is far lower. Code: `pcb/inference/conformal_band.py`, `e1_pcb.py`, `e2_pcb.py`.

### Real-data population conformal band (E1-PCB; 24 pseudo-populations, nominal 90% simultaneous)
| Method | Simultaneous curve coverage | Mean width |
|---|---|---|
| M2 marginal (standard practice) | 37.5% | 0.119 |
| PCB (tightened) | 91.7% | 0.222 |
| PCB marginal (per-threshold) | 98.9% (over-covers, expected) | — |

The standard marginal interval covers the headcount curve simultaneously only 37.5% of the time (nominal 90%). PCB recovers this to 91.7% (finite-sample simultaneous validity), at a cost of width ~1.9x.

### Simulation generalization (E2-PCB)
| sweep | M2 simultaneous | PCB simultaneous | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| K = 3, 5 (high) | 30%, 35% | 100% (width 1.0) | small K gives a trivial band (penalty) |
| K = 10, 20, 50 (high) | 61%, 77%, 81% | 82%, 89%, 89% | PCB approaches nominal as K rises |
| shift = none (K = 20) | 30% | 88% | marginal simultaneous collapses even at zero shift |
| shift low to high | 70% to 77% | 89–93% | gap present throughout, PCB consistent |

**Conclusion:**
1. Under transported distribution functions, marginal conformal severely under-covers the curve simultaneously (real data 37.5%, simulation 30–81%). The collapse occurs even at shift = 0 from the 19-threshold simultaneous condition alone, showing standard curve inference is not valid.
2. PCB (sup-type functional conformal + population exchange) recovers simultaneous coverage to ~90% and approaches nominal as K rises.
3. Caveats: (a) at K ≤ 5, PCB returns a trivial [0,1] band that is valid but uninformative; the small-K penalty applies (the real-data K ≈ 24 is in the useful regime). (b) The shape-compression gain is negligible on this data (~0.2%): the consistency guarantee is free but the empirical benefit is small. (c) PCB width is roughly 2x marginal, the price of simultaneity.

This experiment contributes a population-level simultaneous conformal band for non-smooth transported distribution functions F_new(·), together with the empirical finding of marginal-vs-simultaneous undercoverage. The functional-conformal machinery (sup-score) is applied rather than novel; the contribution is the combination of transported-CDF target, population exchange, and the undercoverage finding.

## Experiment 6 — Localized Conformal Band (M3′, Bias-Corrected): Conditional Efficiency

**Design:** Regress the transport error E_i(·) on population covariate means (closed-form LOO residuals) to predict target bias, re-center the band, then apply conformal to the residuals. Code: `pcb/inference/localized_band.py`, `e_localized.py`.

| Condition | PCB cov/width | LOC cov/width | width LOC/PCB |
|---|---|---|---|
| unstructured (K = 20, high) | 88.0% / 0.462 | 90.7% / 0.532 | 1.15 |
| structured (K = 20) | 84.0% / 0.386 | 90.0% / 0.232 | 0.60 |
| structured (K = 10) | 83.3% / 0.378 | 77.3% / 0.313 | 0.83 |
| structured (K = 50) | 91.3% / 0.380 | 90.7% / 0.198 | 0.52 |

**Conclusion:** Under structured shift with K ≥ 20, the localized band reduces width by 40–48% while maintaining coverage, recovering through re-centering the efficiency gain that the plain weighted conformal band (M3, Experiment 4) could not. Caveats: (a) under unstructured shift the regression fits noise and the band widens (1.15x); (b) at K = 10 it under-covers (77%) due to the LOO approximation and small K. The efficiency contribution is therefore conditional on structured shift and sufficient K.

## Experiment 7 — Targeting Misallocation (E4, simulation)

**Design:** A proxy-means-test scenario: a planner predicts the poverty rate of M = 21 populations and aids the poorest 50%. Point estimates are compared against a valid uncertainty band. Code: `e4_targeting.py`.

| shift | misallocation | regret | honest-flag |
|---|---|---|---|
| none | 26.2% | 0.027 | 4.6% |
| low | 22.3% | 0.075 | 61.5% |
| medium | 21.6% | 0.180 | 72.6% |
| high | 21.4% | 0.335 | 70.2% |

**Conclusion:** Point-estimate targeting wrongly excludes ~22% of genuinely poor populations, and regret rises ~12x with shift. Under shift, 60–73% of populations are statistically indistinguishable at the cutoff (honest-flag), so the planner's confident ranking is illusory. A valid band changes which populations receive aid. This is a simulation result; real-data multi-country validation (Experiment 9) is required.

## Experiment 8 — When Random-Effects Variance Inflation Fails (H2 phase, characterization)

**Design:** Measure the random-effects variance-inflated interval (M1) and PCB coverage over a (|bias|/σ) × (shift) plane. Code: `h2_phase.py`.

| \|bias\|/σ | M1 coverage | PCB coverage |
|---|---|---|
| 0.09–0.28 | 84–89% | 87–91% |
| 0.64 | 86–88% | 91–93% |
| 1.46 | 71.5% | 92.5% |

**Conclusion:** The random-effects variance-inflated interval (M1) under-covers as transport error becomes more biased (|bias|/σ rising), while PCB stays robust. Multilevel models, commonly used for between-group uncertainty, are invalid for simultaneous distribution-function inference under bias. This is the symmetric counterpart to Experiment 2's naive collapse: M1 captures variance but not bias.

The combined picture across Experiments 5–8 maps onto the paper's three contributions: C1, the diagnosis of marginal-vs-simultaneous undercoverage together with the M1-invalidity characterization; C2, the PCB method with its localized and covariate-shift refinements; and C3, the audit / survey-allocation result.

## Experiment 7-R — Targeting on Real Data (E4 reality check, null result)

**Design:** Anchor the simulated E4 in real data. Rank the 24 pseudo-populations by (a) pseudo-population LOPO prediction and (b) leave-one-survey-out genuine-shift prediction. Code: `e4_real.py`, `e4_real_loso.py`.

| Prediction | policy line | misallocation | regret | honest-flag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| pseudo-pop LOPO | 5.88 / 9.13 / 14.03 | 0% / 0% / 0% | 0 | 29 / 13 / 42% |
| LOSO genuine shift | 5.88 / 9.13 / 14.03 | 0% / 0% / 0% | 0 | 25 / 13 / 38% |

**Conclusion:** On real data, misallocation = 0%. The poverty differences between pseudo-populations are much larger than the transport error, so even under genuine shift (LOSO) the point-estimate ranking matches the true ranking. The honest-flag (13–42%) reveals near-cutoff uncertainty without changing the ranking.

Implication: the E4 policy payoff (22% misallocation, 12x regret in simulation) is simulation-specific and is not reproduced on this real data. The mechanism is visible but the real-data evidence is null. Demonstrating a substantive payoff requires external multi-country data (Experiment 9) where shift is as large as the poverty differences; three-survey data is insufficient.

## Experiment 9 — Real-Data Multi-Country Validation (E3, World Bank PIP)

**Data:** Poverty curves for 2,475 country-year surveys (10 poverty lines, \$1–\$30) from the PIP API, plus covariates (mean, gini, decile). Code: `pcb/data/fetch_pip.py`, `e3_pip.py`. Population = country-year; plug-in F̂ = lognormal(mean, gini); leave-one-country-out (genuine cross-country shift).

### Consumption (955 populations, 123 countries, 10 thresholds)
| | Simultaneous curve coverage | width |
|---|---|---|
| M2 marginal (standard) | 54.1% | 0.060 |
| PCB | 88.9% (marginal 98.5%) | 0.097 |

### E4 targeting (93 countries, poorest quartile @ \$6.85)
misallocation 4.3%, regret 0.031.

**Conclusion:**
1. The finding generalizes to real data: marginal bands cover the 123-country curve simultaneously only 54% of the time (nominal 90%); it is not a simulation artifact.
2. PCB recovers simultaneous coverage under real cross-country shift, reaching 88.9% at K = 123 countries.
3. Real-data E4 misallocation is nonzero (4.3%), in contrast to the pseudo-population 0%; genuine cross-country shift produces some wrongful exclusion.

Caveat: the lognormal plug-in fits well (MAE 0.013), so transport error is small and misallocation is modest (4.3%, not the simulated 22%). A stronger payoff requires a harder transport that predicts without a target survey (covariates only), increasing the shift.

## Experiment 9-T — Genuine Transport, Survey-less Poverty Nowcasting (E3)

**Design:** The Experiment 9 plug-in used the target country's own survey mean/gini, so transport error was small (MAE 0.013). Here the target is not surveyed: mean and gini are predicted from log(GDP) + region + year (trained on other countries), then fed to the lognormal plug-in. This matches the policy reality of nowcasting poverty for countries without a recent survey. Code: `e3_transport.py`.

| (123 countries, leave-one-country-out) | Simultaneous curve coverage | width |
|---|---|---|
| transported plug-in MAE | 0.085 (6.5x the own-survey value) | — |
| M2 marginal (standard) | 60.3% | 0.417 |
| PCB | 89.1% | 0.486 |
| E4 targeting (108 countries, poorest quartile @ \$6.85) | misallocation 25.9% | regret 0.979 |

**Conclusion:**
1. With real data and large shift, both the finding and the method hold: marginal under-covers at 60% simultaneous, PCB recovers to 89%.
2. The E4 payoff appears on real data (25.9% misallocation), in contrast to pseudo-population 0% and own-survey 4.3%. Under genuine transport (countries without a survey), point estimation wrongly excludes a quarter of the poorest countries, comparable to the simulated 22%.
3. Estimating poverty from macro covariates for countries without a recent survey is standard practice; point estimates wrongly exclude 26% while appearing reliable, and PCB exposes this through a valid band.

| PCB — simultaneous curve coverage | 89.8% (width 0.276) |

Combined with consumption (Experiment 9-T), the marginal-vs-simultaneous undercoverage and PCB's recovery hold across both PIP welfare definitions.

## Experiment 10 — Two Adversarial Tests of the Payoff

Two adversarial tests of the targeting payoff. Code: `test1_realistic_baseline.py`, `test2_decision_value.py`.

### Test 1 — Does misallocation survive a realistic nowcasting baseline?
| Baseline | MAE | PCB cov | M2 simultaneous | misalloc % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B0 grand-mean | 0.195 | 90.1% | 74.7% | 100% |
| B1 GDP linear (crude) | 0.085 | 89.1% | 60.3% | 25.9% |
| B2 lagged-survey (WB standard nowcast) | 0.035 | 89.7% | 65.1% | 12.5% |
| B3 GBM (strong ML) | 0.050 | 89.5% | 65.1% | 18.5% |

**Result:** The 26% figure is partly a product of a weak baseline; under a strong nowcaster it falls to 12.5%, but it is not zero (12–18%). PCB coverage (~89%) and the finding (M2 simultaneous 60–75%, below nominal) are robust across all baselines, so the method is independent of predictor quality. The payoff is real but modest.

### Test 2 — Does using the valid band improve decisions (audit allocation)?
| audit budget | POINT regret | BAND regret | improvement |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–10% | 0.979 | 0.979 | 0% |
| 20% | 0.979 | 0.887 | 9.3% |
| 30% | 0.979 | 0.614 | 37.2% |

**Result:** The link from a valid band to better policy is demonstrated (regret −37%), but only at larger audit budgets; at low budgets there is no gain (no confidence-poverty cases, bands wide).

### Combined assessment
Both tests pass, and both are modest. The headline 26% depends on a weak baseline; the accurate summary is that realistic poverty nowcasting still wrongly excludes 12–18% of the poorest countries, marginal bands undercover the curve, and PCB provides a valid simultaneous band robustly and independently of the predictor. The contribution has three parts: C1, the diagnosis of marginal-vs-simultaneous undercoverage with the M1-invalidity characterization; C2, the PCB method with its localized and covariate-shift refinements; and C3, the audit / survey-allocation result, backed by real-data multi-country validation (123 countries) and a policy demonstration.
